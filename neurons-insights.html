Neurons and Interesting Insights

A reflection on the architecture of the human mind, in the spirit of resilience, curiosity, and hope

The Long Road Inside the Mind

I remember a time when the greatest journey I could imagine was measured in footsteps across a sun-baked plain, not in the microscopic leaps of electricity across a web of living tissue. Yet I have come to understand that the longest walk any of us ever undertakes is not the one that takes us over mountains or through prison gates — it is the quiet, continuous journey that unfolds inside our own minds, carried forward by billions upon billions of tiny messengers we call neurons.

Each neuron is both an individual and a citizen. It lives its own brief life, no longer than a few decades at most, and yet it does not walk alone. It reaches out to its neighbours with slender arms called dendrites, and when a message is worth sharing, it sends a spark along a thread called the axon, arcing across a gap so narrow it defies imagination — a gap called the synapse. In that gap, the past meets the future. In that gap, learning becomes possible.

"Education is the most powerful weapon which you can use to change the world." — Nelson Mandela

There is a profound lesson here, one that echoes through every classroom and every act of teaching: when a neuron fires, it does so not for itself alone, but for the network. The strength of its signal depends on the bonds it has forged. The more two neurons communicate, the stronger their connection grows. What is true in the brain is true among us as well. We become strong not by standing apart, but by reaching out, again and again, until our connections are forged in the fire of shared experience.

Interesting Insights from the Science of Neurons

1. The Brain Rewrites Itself Every Day

  • Scientists call this neuroplasticity. Your brain does not remain fixed like a statue carved in stone. It rewires itself based on what you learn, what you practice, and even what you imagine.
  • A violinist who practises for years will grow thicker neural pathways in the regions that control the fingers. A person who learns a new language will reshape the circuits that process sound and meaning.
  • The lesson is clear: it is never too late to learn. It is never too late to change the way you think.

2. A Single Neuron Can Connect to Thousands of Others

  • Each neuron in your cerebral cortex can form up to 10,000 synaptic connections. The total number of connections in an adult human brain exceeds 100 trillion.
  • This is not chaos. It is organise complexity. Out of this web emerges consciousness, memory, creativity, and the quiet voice we call conscience.

3. Your Neurons Communicate with Chemical Poetry

  • When a signal reaches the end of an axon, it does not simply leap across the gap. It triggers the release of neurotransmitters — tiny chemical messengers such as dopamine, serotonin, and glutamate.
  • These molecules drift across the synapse like words carried on a breeze, binding to receptors on the neighbouring neuron. Joy, sorrow, focus, and fear — all begin as chemistry, yet they feel like the whole of life itself.

4. Your Brain Uses Less Power Than a Light Bulb

  • Despite its staggering complexity, the human brain operates on roughly 20 watts of power — less than the bulb above your desk. It achieves this through elegant efficiency: most neurons are silent most of the time, firing only when needed.
  • There is wisdom here. The most powerful voices are often the quietest. The most effective work is done not by shouting, but by knowing precisely when to speak.

The Frontal Lobe and the Dream of What Is Possible

If you place your hand upon your forehead, you are touching the very region of your brain that makes you most human. The prefrontal cortex sits behind the brow like a quiet general, orchestrating planning, self-control, empathy, and moral reasoning. It is the last part of the brain to mature — not fully developed until the mid-twenties — and it is the first to suffer when we are exhausted, afraid, or starved of hope.

I have spent many years reflecting on what it means to lead. I have learned that leadership is not the art of commanding others, but the art of seeing what others have not yet seen, and holding fast to that vision until it becomes real. The prefrontal cortex does exactly this. It holds an imagined future in working memory, and it guides the body and the rest of the brain toward that future, one decision at a time. Without this capacity, we would act only on impulse, never on principle.

"It always seems impossible until it is done." — Nelson Mandela

When scientists study people who have suffered damage to the prefrontal cortex, they observe a tragic pattern. These individuals may retain their intelligence, their knowledge, and their skills, yet they lose the ability to choose wisely. They become slaves to the immediate moment. They cannot hold the long road in mind. And so I say to you: the health of this small region of brain tissue is, in a very real sense, the health of your character. Nurture it with sleep, with reflection, with the discipline to pause before acting, and with the habit of asking not only "what do I want?" but "what is right?"

Glial Cells — The Unsung Heroes

For too long, scientists celebrated the neuron and ignored its quieter companions. But the brain is not merely a network of electrically chattering cells. It is also a community supported by glial cells — cells that do not carry signals in the way neurons do, yet without which the neurons would starve, suffocate, and drown in their own waste.

The astrocytes feed the neurons with nutrients from the blood. The microglia patrol like immune soldiers, pruning weak connections and clearing debris. The oligodendrocytes wrap the axons in fatty insulation called myelin, allowing signals to travel up to a hundred times faster. This is the work of the invisible, the uncelebrated, the background labour upon which all visible achievement rests.

And so I must ask you, as I have often asked myself: in your own life, who are the glial cells? Who feeds you, clears your path, insulates you from the cold, and asks for nothing in return? Honour them. For without them, the brightest mind falters, and the longest road becomes impassable.

The Synapse as a Metaphor for Reconciliation

There is one more thing I wish to share with you, and it is perhaps the most important. When two neurons meet at a synapse, they do not touch. There is always a gap. The signal must cross this gap as a chemical whisper, received and transformed on the other side. If the gap were not there, the brain would short-circuit. The very space between us — the space that seems like separation — is actually the condition for communication.

I have stood in rooms where the gap between people seemed wider than any ocean. I have seen hatred calcify into walls thicker than stone. And yet I have also seen what happens when one side sends a signal across that gap: a word of respect, a gesture of acknowledgment, a willingness to listen. The other side may not receive it at first. The receptors may be blocked by fear or pride. But if the signal is repeated, if it is sincere, the chemistry changes. New receptors grow. The gap becomes a bridge.

"No one is born hating another person because of the colour of his skin, or his background, or his religion. People must learn to hate, and if they can learn to hate, they can be taught to love, for love comes more naturally to the human heart than its opposite." — Nelson Mandela, Long Walk to Freedom

A Final Word on the Long Walk

The human brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons. Each one is a small, fragile thing, living and dying in relative obscurity. None of them, in isolation, could produce a thought, a memory, or a dream. Yet together, bound by trillions of synapses, they generate the very consciousness that allows us to read these words, to feel wonder, to plan for tomorrow, and to forgive yesterday.

I have walked many roads in my life — roads of dust, of concrete, of despair, and of hope. I have learned that the longest road is not measured in kilometres, but in the willingness to keep walking when every step costs more than the last. The neurons in your brain walk such a road every second of every day. They fire, they rest, they rewire, they endure. They do not know defeat, because they do not know how to stop reaching out.

May we be worthy of their example. May we fire when it is time to act. May we rest when it is time to reflect. May we rewire ourselves when old patterns no longer serve the world we wish to build. And may we never stop reaching across the gaps — between neuron and neuron, between person and person, between what is and what could be.

"I have walked that long road to freedom. I have tried not to falter; I have made missteps along the way. But I have discovered the secret that after climbing a great hill, one only finds that there are many more hills to climb." — Nelson Mandela, Long Walk to Freedom

The journey of the neuron is the journey of us all. Let us walk it together, with humility, with courage, and with an unshakeable faith in what we might yet become.
